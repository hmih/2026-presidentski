## Гюров

Gyurov debates as a technocratic incumbent-in-waiting: almost every exchange is converted into a comparison between the 78-day caretaker cabinet he ran and the Radev-Iotova record, and he keeps three numbers in circulation - half a million a day for Botash, 6.8 billion euro left in the fiscal reserve, and not a single binding sentence in the Kyiv agreement. His strongest register is security, where he moves from international-law doctrine at 07:24 to casualty lists and an accusation of passivity against Iotova as commander-in-chief. His weakest register is the personal: the pardon attack, which he insists is not personal, sits beside complaints about trolls, collages and a dirty campaign. He consistently refuses to ask for more presidential powers while arguing that the office must become a counterweight, which lets him answer the authoritarianism charge with self-limitation instead of denial. The closing frame is vindication - a positive civic campaign against an opponent's campaign of fear - so the performance is disciplined and number-heavy, more comfortable prosecuting a record than describing a programme.

### Name the aggressor and arm the coast - silence is not defence

- **Problem:** Bulgarian security has deteriorated - explosions on Bulgarian soil, fallen drones, sunk ships and dead sailors, with the Black Sea less safe than the Persian Gulf - because the state lowered its profile instead of naming the threat.
- **Culprit:** Iotova and Radev's presidential institution, which kept silent, refused the NATO drone programme over a 220,000-euro price tag and convened the Consultative Council only after weeks of visible escalation.
- **Remedy:** Name Russia as the aggressor, join EU and NATO defence schemes early, build deterrence so the cost of attack becomes too high, and co-develop the technology at home instead of buying it late.
- **Warning:** A wish for a moratorium is not a defence strategy, and silence is not defence.

## Йотова

iotova runs a two-track performance: a first half built on sovereign dignity, security credentials and the Black Sea, and a second half in which almost every attack is turned into a mirror of the opponent's own record. Her method is to accept the frame, concede a detail, and then overwhelm it with first-person authority - 'I was there', 'I asked ten people', 'I signed it after two and a half hours of listening' - backed by a specific date or document. The Kyiv agreement is her central weapon: she returns to the missing mandate, the ten-year term and the Belene reactors at least four times, pairing it with an absolute pledge that no Bulgarian soldier will set foot in Ukraine. From roughly minute 60 she is on the defensive over the budget and the pardons, and she answers by relocating the burden of proof to the Constitutional Court, the prosecution and Vasilev's 2024 paperwork. The closing ten minutes abandon argument for biography, family and 1 November, softening a performance that had been combative and, in the pardon exchange, openly personal.
